The court is one of the most memorable parts of a quinceañera. The damas and chambelanes walk in with the quinceañera, dance the vals beside her, and usually bring the energy for the surprise dance. They're also a group of teenagers with busy schedules, which means they take some planning.

Here's how to put together a court, keep rehearsals on track, and make sure everyone knows where to be on the day.

Choosing the court

There's no required size. Some quinceañeras have a full court of damas and chambelanes, others have only chambelanes, and some keep it to a chambelán de honor. Think about what feels right for your daughter and what you can realistically coordinate.

A few things to consider:

  • Reliability matters more than numbers. Choose kids who will actually show up to rehearsals. One missing pair can throw off a whole formation.
  • Talk to parents early. Being in a court is a commitment of time and, often, money. Families should know that before saying yes.
  • Keep the dance floor in mind. A large court needs space for the vals. Check your venue's floor size before you settle on a number.
  • Let your daughter lead. These are her friends and cousins. Her input makes the court feel like hers.

The chambelán de honor

The chambelán de honor is the quinceañera's main escort. He walks in with her, dances closest to her in the vals, and is usually part of the most photographed moments of the night.

Many families choose a close friend, cousin, or brother. Whoever it is, he should be comfortable dancing in front of a crowd and committed to every rehearsal. It's also kind to talk with his family about what's expected before asking.

Rehearsals for the vals and surprise dance

Dances are where most court stress comes from, and most of it is avoidable with a steady schedule.

Start early

Begin rehearsals a few months before the event. Weekly practice gives everyone time to learn the steps without cramming. If you're hiring a choreographer, ask how many sessions they recommend and what they need from you (space, music, a final count of pairs).

Set expectations up front

  • Share the full rehearsal schedule with every family at the start.
  • Be clear about what happens if someone misses several practices.
  • Name one adult as the contact for schedule changes.

Plan the final rehearsal

Hold a full run-through the week of the event, ideally with the real music and shoes. If you can rehearse at the venue, even better, but a space of similar size works too.

Don't forget the surprise dance outfit change

The surprise dance usually happens after a quick change. Practice the change itself, not just the dance. Know where the court will change, where their outfits will be, and how long it should take.

Attire

Decide on the court's attire early so families have time to buy, rent, or alter clothes.

  • Damas: Agree on color, length, and style. Some families pick one dress for everyone, while others choose a color and let each dama find her own.
  • Chambelanes: Suits, tuxedos, or charro-inspired outfits are all common. Decide on accessories like ties, vests, or sombreros so everything matches.
  • Surprise dance: Many courts wear a second, more casual or themed outfit. Plan this alongside the first so families aren't hit with a late request.
  • Shoes: Make sure everyone practices in the shoes they'll wear.

Be upfront about who is paying for what. Clear expectations prevent awkward conversations later.

Communicating with court parents

Court parents are your partners in this. Keeping them informed makes everything smoother.

  • Use one channel. A group text or chat keeps updates in one place.
  • Send a written summary. Rehearsal dates, attire details, costs, and the day-of schedule, all in one message they can refer back to.
  • Give advance notice of changes. If a rehearsal moves, let families know as early as possible.
  • Share the day-of call times. About two weeks out, send exactly when and where each court member needs to be.

Day-of cues for the court

On the day, the court has several moments where they need to be in the right place at the right time. A simple plan helps:

  • Call time: When to arrive dressed and ready, for both the Misa and the reception.
  • Misa: Where they sit and whether they process in with the quinceañera. Our post on planning the Misa de Acción de Gracias covers the church side.
  • Photos: Which groupings they're in, so they don't wander off after Mass.
  • Grand entrance: The order of pairs and where they stop on the dance floor.
  • Vals: When to line up, usually a song or two before it starts.
  • Outfit change: When to leave the room and how long they have.
  • Surprise dance: When to be back and lined up.

Give the court a one-page version of these cues. Teenagers don't need the full timeline, just their part. For the bigger planning picture, see our month-by-month planning timeline.

Who manages the court on the day?

This is the part that's hardest for parents to do themselves. You'll want to be with your daughter, greeting family, and enjoying the night, not rounding up chambelanes from the parking lot.
