A Mexican wedding is full of moments that carry generations of meaning. Some happen at the altar, some on the dance floor, and some the next day. Every family does them a little differently depending on where they come from, their faith, and what feels right to the couple.

Here is what the most common traditions mean, what each one needs to go smoothly, and how a coordinator keeps them on cue. Choose the ones that fit your story.

Padrinos and madrinas

Padrinos are the sponsors who walk alongside the couple, spiritually and often practically. There are usually padrinos de velación or de honor, who serve as mentors in the marriage, plus padrinos who sponsor specific items: the lazo, the arras, the rings, the Bible and rosary, the cake, the mariachi.

Planning tip: keep a written list of every padrino, what they are giving, and whether they have a role in the ceremony. Each one should know when to come forward, what to carry, and where to sit. If your ceremony is in a Catholic church, ask the parish how they handle padrinos' roles, since practices vary.

On the day: I check in the padrinos when they arrive, confirm they have their items, and line them up in order before the ceremony begins.

Las arras

The arras are thirteen coins, blessed and given by the groom to the bride, often presented by padrinos in a small box or tray. They represent the couple's commitment to care for their home and family together. Thirteen is often said to stand for Christ and the twelve apostles.

Planning tip: decide who carries the arras to the altar and who keeps them afterward. Practice the exchange at the rehearsal, because coins in nervous hands can end up on the floor.

El lazo

After the vows, padrinos place a lazo, a large rosary or a cord of flowers or silk, in a figure eight over the couple's shoulders. It symbolizes their union and the bond they are entering together. The lazo stays on through a portion of the ceremony and is removed by the padrinos.

Planning tip: confirm with your officiant exactly when the lazo is placed and removed, and make sure the padrinos know both cues. Tell your photographer too. It is one of the most photographed moments of the ceremony.

The Bible, rosary, and other blessed items

Many couples also receive a Bible and a rosary from padrinos, sometimes along with a cojín for kneeling. These are often blessed during the Mass.

Planning tip: keep these items together in one labeled box so nothing is forgotten in the car or left behind in a pew.

Mariachi and the serenata

Mariachi might play during the ceremony, at the cocktail hour, or as a surprise at the reception. Some families also keep the tradition of a serenata, a serenade, for the bride.

Planning tip: ask your church and venue whether live musicians are allowed and where they can set up. Give the mariachi a clear arrival time and a short list of songs.

The first dance and family dances

After the couple's first dance, many weddings include dances with parents, padrinos, or grandparents. Some couples dance a vals, which connects nicely to the quinceañera tradition many brides grew up with.

Planning tip: give your DJ the exact songs, versions, and start and stop points ahead of time, plus the names of everyone being called to the floor.

El baile del billete (the money dance)

During the money dance, guests pin bills to the couple's clothing or drop them in a basket for a turn to dance with the bride or groom. It is a fun, joyful way for guests to help the newlyweds start their life together.

Planning tip: decide whether you want it at all, since some couples skip it. If you do, choose someone trusted to collect the money, have safety pins or a basket ready, and set a time limit so it does not take over the dance floor.

On the day: I work with the DJ to start and close the money dance on schedule and make sure the money goes to the person you chose.

La víbora de la mar

La víbora de la mar is a lively game set to a traditional song. The couple stands on chairs and forms a bridge, sometimes holding the bride's veil or a scarf between them, while guests form a line and weave underneath. The line speeds up, the couple tries to stay on their chairs, and everyone laughs. Often the women go first and then the men.

Planning tip: pick sturdy chairs and assign a few strong friends to steady them. Make sure the bride's dress is bustled first. It is energetic, so plan it for when guests are warmed up but before people start heading home.

The cake, the toast, and la hora loca

Cutting the cake and toasting the couple are familiar at most weddings. Many Mexican-American receptions also add a hora loca, a high-energy segment with props, costumes, and music that brings everyone back to the floor late in the night.

Planning tip: confirm who will speak during the toast and in which language, and ask the catering or bar team to pour for every table before it starts. If you book a hora loca, make sure it fits within the venue's music curfew.

La tornaboda

The tornaboda means different things in different families. For some it is the gathering the day after the wedding, with leftovers, recalentado, and family who did not want the party to end. For others it is the late-night continuation after the main reception.

Planning tip: if the tornaboda is the next day, decide where it will be, who is hosting, and what food and rentals need to move there from the venue. Assign someone to take home leftovers, centerpieces, and gifts at the end of the reception.

Putting the traditions in order

There is no single correct order. A common flow looks something like this, but couples rearrange it all the time:

  1. Ceremony, with the arras, the lazo, and blessed items
  2. Cocktail hour, sometimes with mariachi
  3. Grand entrance of the wedding party and the couple
  4. Dinner and toast
  5. First dance and family dances
  6. Money dance
  7. Cake cutting
  8. Víbora de la mar and open dancing, then hora loca

A few things help the night flow: feed guests before the high-energy moments, keep the dances grouped together, and share the order with your photographer so they are in position for each tradition.
